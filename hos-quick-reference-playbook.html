Staff Wellness & Stability Plan · 2026–2027

"Keep this somewhere you’ll actually reach for it."

How to Use This Playbook

Fast, practical reference for the situations you face every day.

This playbook translates the Staff Wellness & Stability Guiding Principles into fast, practical reference for the situations you face every day. Each tab answers four questions:

When does this apply? · What do I do? · What do I say? · When do I call HR? Find the tab for the situation you’re in right now. You don’t need to read this cover to cover.

Gold-tinted boxes throughout this playbook connect each situation to the underlying Guiding Principle it reflects. When you have more time, reading those principles provides the ‘why’ behind the ‘what’ in this playbook.

Rule of Thumb

If you are unsure whether a situation requires HR involvement, it does. Contact HR before making formal accommodations, adjustments, or communications in any support situation.

01

Leadership Foundations & Daily Practice

Your core commitments, alignment rules, and operating rhythm.

Guiding Principles 1, 2, 4

Staff wellness and student success are inseparable. You lead with clarity, not control. Trust is earned through consistency.

Your Commitments to Staff

  • Communicate clearly, honestly, and consistently — with staff and with each other
  • Explain the reasoning behind decisions that affect staff schedules, workload, or responsibilities
  • Protect planning time and proactively reduce unnecessary stressors
  • Respond to concerns early — before they escalate
  • Foster an environment where staff feel safe speaking up without fear of retaliation
  • Apply expectations consistently and fairly across all staff
  • Follow through on every commitment — or explain honestly why you didn’t

Alignment Rules

  • Align with other leaders before decisions that significantly affect staff — staff should never receive contradictory guidance
  • If you’re unsure of the aligned position, ask before you answer
  • Accountability to each other on the leadership team is part of the commitment to staff

Teacher Autonomy — What You Protect

Within established priorities, staff exercise professional judgment in:

  • Instructional strategies and classroom delivery methods
  • Classroom culture and relationship-building approaches
  • Lesson pacing based on what students need
  • Professional growth goals and instructional self-reflection

Coach to clarity, not compliance for its own sake. Distinguish between school aligned instructional expectations — which are non-negotiable and worth coaching directly — and areas where teachers have genuine professional latitude. When staff understand what is required versus what is theirs to own, morale and investment follow. Clarity about non-negotiables is what makes real autonomy possible.

Your Operating Rhythm

Every Week

  • Communicate priorities clearly — no vague or last-minute direction
  • Send 2–3 specific, personal appreciation messages
  • Check in with any staff member who may be struggling
  • Protect all scheduled planning periods
  • Complete self-assessment (or bi-weekly minimum)
  • End all meetings on time

Every Month

  • Review your self-assessment trends — what’s your pattern?
  • Execute one culture recognition moment (see Tab 15)
  • Confirm nothing was added to staff without removing something first
  • Check for emerging burnout or morale patterns

Twice a Year (October & February)

  • Complete stay conversations with all staff (Tab 6)
  • Submit theme summary to HR — Nov 1 (R1) and Mar 1 (R2)
02

Workload & Initiative Management

Before adding any new work — use this tab.

Guiding Principle 3

We protect people’s time as seriously as we protect their dignity.

Before Launching Anything New

  • Does it align with one of this year’s schoolwide priorities?
  • What is being removed or paused to make room for it?
  • Does it land during a high-stress period? If yes — hold it.
  • Do staff have adequate training and lead time?
  • Has the leadership team aligned before it’s communicated to staff?

High-Stress Periods — Standing Response

PeriodYour Standing Response
Start of yearLimit new initiatives; focus on clarity and classroom readiness
Testing windowsReduce nonessential meetings; no new work assigned
Report card / gradingProtect grading time; minimize competing deadlines
Parent conferencesLimit additional evening obligations where possible
Compliance deadlines / auditsProvide administrative support; communicate requirements early
Final weeks of yearReduce nonessential tasks; focus on student closeout and transition

Non-Negotiable Rules

  • Nothing is added without something being removed or paused first
  • No major initiative during a high-stress window without leadership team approval
  • All scheduled planning periods protected from non-urgent interruption
  • Advance notice required when planning time must be repurposed

Initiative overload is a leadership failure, not a staff problem. If staff are overwhelmed, look first at the volume and timing of demands placed on them.

What to Say — When Staff Raise Workload Concerns

Staff: I feel like I’m drowning — there’s too much on my plate right now.

You: Tell me specifically what’s hitting hardest. I want to understand what can wait and what can’t — let’s figure that out together.

03

Communication & Work-Life Boundaries

After-hours messages, meetings, and planning time protection.

Guiding Principle 3

We protect people’s time as seriously as we protect their dignity.

After-Hours Communication Norms

  • Routine messages sent after hours → staff respond the next workday
  • Urgent messages must explicitly state they are urgent and name the expected response
  • Urgent = safety, emergency, unexpected closure. Not scheduling, feedback, or reminders.
  • You set the norm by how you communicate. If you send at 9pm and don’t need a reply — say so.

Every communication should include: purpose, required action, deadline, and contact for questions.

Meeting Standards

  • Agendas distributed before every meeting
  • Meetings end on time — every time
  • Scheduled during normal work hours whenever possible
  • If it could be an email — make it an email

Planning Time

  • Scheduled planning periods are protected — do not repurpose without advance notice
  • Track how often planning time is interrupted — patterns are a system problem to address
What to Say — Staff Feels Expected to Respond at Night

You: That’s not the expectation. Unless I mark something urgent, everything I send after hours waits until your next workday. You are not expected to respond to me outside work hours. I should have made that clearer.

What to Say — Planning Time Keeps Getting Interrupted

You: That’s a problem — your planning time is protected. Tell me which days this happened and I will make sure it’s addressed. It should not be routine.

04

Leave & Coverage Decisions

Necessary leave, voluntary leave, and coverage planning.

Guiding Principle 6

We respond to struggle with care before we respond with process.

Necessary Leave

Includes: illness, medical needs, bereavement, family emergencies, required appointments, unexpected significant circumstances.

→ Support the staff member. Arrange coverage. Minimize disruption to students.

Voluntary Leave — Review Factors

  • Timing within the school calendar — testing? grading? parent conferences?
  • Length of absence and coverage availability
  • Impact on students and instructional continuity
  • Nature and significance of the personal request
  • Effect on colleagues and fairness across the team

School breaks are the expected time for discretionary leave. Routine school-year voluntary leave places strain on students and colleagues. Always explore partial accommodations before a full denial. Never make staff feel ashamed for asking.

How to Respond to Any Leave Request

  • Acknowledge with respect — no sighing, no visible judgment
  • Review operational impact before committing to an answer
  • Explore partial accommodations before a full denial
  • Communicate your decision clearly, kindly, and promptly
What to Say — Approval

You: I can make this work. Here’s what I’ll need from you before you leave: [lesson plans, coverage notes, student information]. Have a great trip.

What to Say — Denial, With a Partial Option

You: Those dates overlap with [testing/grading] and I can’t approve that window for everyone. Before I give you a firm answer — tell me which specific days matter most. I want to find a partial option if one exists.

Coverage Planning

  • Secure substitutes when available; rotate internal coverage fairly when not
  • Do not repeatedly overburden the same individuals with coverage
  • Require advance preparation (lesson plans, student notes) from any approved leave
Call HR

Contact HR before approving any extended or FMLA-adjacent leave request.

05

Psychological Safety & Feedback

When staff raise concerns, push back, or share critical feedback.

Guiding Principle 5

We make it safe to tell the truth.

What Psychological Safety Requires From You

  • Listen actively — do not pre-decide or minimize
  • Respond calmly, even when the feedback is directly about your leadership
  • Do not conflate professional disagreement with disloyalty
  • Acknowledge when an adjustment or improvement is appropriate
  • Follow up on every concern — with action or an honest explanation of why nothing changed
  • Never retaliate against, penalize, or cool toward a staff member who raises a concern

If you feel defensive about feedback — pause before responding. Your reaction in that moment determines whether staff will raise the next concern. Defensiveness teaches staff that honesty here is not safe.

Anti-Retaliation — Non-Negotiable

Staff must not face adverse treatment for:

  • Asking questions or seeking clarification
  • Respectfully disagreeing with a decision
  • Participating in surveys, listening sessions, or feedback opportunities
  • Raising concerns through any appropriate channel

Feedback Channels — Make Sure Staff Know These

  • Direct conversation with you
  • Human Resources — no explanation required to access
  • Anonymous feedback tools when available
  • Listening sessions scheduled by leadership
What to Say — Staff Raises a Concern About a Decision

Staff: Decisions get made around here and we find out about them after the fact.

You: Thank you for saying that directly. Give me a specific example — I want to understand where the breakdown happened.

Staff: The schedule change in October. We found out the day it happened.

You: That’s fair, and that should not have landed that way. Here’s what I’m going to commit to: any schedule change that affects your planning time comes to you from me before it’s announced to anyone else. I’m writing that down now.

What to Say — When You Can’t Change a Decision

You: I hear you — and I’m not able to change this decision. Here’s why it was made: [reason]. I know that’s not what you wanted to hear. What I can do is [what you can offer]. I’m glad you told me.

06

Stay Conversations

October (Round 1) and February (Round 2) — plus as needed throughout the year.

Guiding Principle 9

We stay curious about our people — and use what we learn.

Before the Conversation

  • Review what you know about this staff member’s year so far — workload, any concerns, positive moments
  • Block 30–45 minutes; choose a private setting with no interruptions
  • Remind yourself: this is a listening conversation, not an evaluation
  • Identify 1–2 things you are genuinely curious about — not just covering questions

Core Questions

  • What aspects of your work are going well this year?
  • What feels most challenging right now?
  • Are there obstacles making your job harder than it needs to be?
  • What support from me has been most helpful?
  • What could I do differently to better support you?
  • What keeps you motivated to continue working here?
  • What would make this a place where you want to stay long term?

Always close with: ‘Is there anything I didn’t ask that you want me to know?’

During the Conversation — Rules

  • Ask, then be quiet. Do not fill silences — let them think.
  • Ask follow-up questions before moving to the next topic
  • If something surprises you: ‘Thank you for telling me that’ — not defensiveness
  • Do not make promises in the moment — say ‘I’ll think about that and follow up’
What to Say — If Staff Are Hesitant

You: I want you to know this conversation stays between us. I’m not sharing specific things you tell me — I’m looking for patterns to improve how I lead. Nothing you say here will get you in trouble.

What to Say — If Staff Critique Your Leadership

You: I appreciate you saying that directly. That’s exactly what I need to hear. Can you give me a specific example so I understand where it’s been happening?

Staff: When [example].

You: That’s fair. I’m going to make a specific commitment about that. And I want you to hold me to it.

After the Conversation

  • Document themes — not direct quotes — within 24 hours while they’re fresh
  • Calendar any follow-up commitment immediately — before you close your notes
  • Flag recurring themes for the leadership team
  • Submit theme summary to HR — Nov 1 (Round 1) or Mar 1 (Round 2)

If a staff member raises something about your leadership — that’s the most valuable data in the room. Receive it without defense.

07

Burnout Recognition & Response

When you notice a staff member struggling — act early.

Guiding Principles 7 & 9

We treat mental health with the same respect as physical health. We stay curious about our people.

Signs to Watch For

CategoryWhat You May Observe
Energy & EngagementPersistent fatigue; reduced enthusiasm; increased negativity about routine work
Work PatternsMissed deadlines; declining preparation quality; difficulty staying organized
Emotional IndicatorsWithdrawal from colleagues; irritability; expressed feeling of being overwhelmed
AttendanceIncreased sick days; frequent late arrivals; requests for extended time away

Patterns over time signal burnout — not single incidents. When you see a pattern, act.

Step-by-Step Response

  • Schedule a private check-in — frame it as care, not a performance conversation
  • Listen first; understand what’s actually driving the strain before suggesting solutions
  • Help clarify priorities — what can wait right now?
  • Consider temporary workload adjustments
  • Mention EAP and wellness resources without pressure or judgment
  • Set a follow-up check-in within two weeks
What to Say — Opening

You: I’ve noticed you seem to be carrying more than usual lately. I’m not here to check on your performance — I just want to make sure you’re okay and see if there’s anything I can take off your plate.

Staff: I’m fine, it’s just been a busy stretch.

You: I hear that. Can I ask a couple of questions just to make sure we’re setting you up well?

What to Say — When Work Is the Source

You: What’s been most disruptive — is it the volume, the timing, or something specific? I want to understand what I can actually do about it.

What to Say — Connecting to Resources

You: If anything outside work is contributing to this, the EAP is completely confidential and free. I’m not saying something is wrong — I just want you to know it’s there before you might need it.

Call HR

When burnout appears across a team or role: look at workload, initiative pacing, and coverage demands before drawing conclusions about individuals. Contact HR.

Grief Support

Death of a spouse, child, immediate family member, or close household member.

Guiding Principle 6

We respond to struggle with care before we respond with process.

Full protocol: Appendix A of the Wellness Plan.

Immediate Response

  • Reach out personally — not by email — as soon as you learn of the loss
  • Express care without operational agenda: this conversation is not about coverage
  • Ask what the staff member wants their colleagues to know — and honor that exactly
  • Contact HR immediately to coordinate leave, coverage, and workload adjustments

Sped / MLLNon-urgent IEPs within compliance windows; non-critical progress reportsCase management help; clerical support; admin monitors legal timelines
Office / OperationsEvent planning; procurement; inventory; front-desk coverage blocksEssential reporting only; backup for attendance and transportation

Across all roles: non-compliance meetings optional, flexible hours, no coverage asks, parent emails rerouted, removed from test proctoring, excused from public events.

Check-In Rhythm

PeriodFrequency
Week 1Several times daily
Week 2Twice daily
Week 3Once daily
After Week 3As needed — jointly determined
What to Say — First Conversation

You: I am so sorry for your loss. Please don’t worry about work right now — everything here is covered. When you’re ready to think about returning, I’m here and we’ll make it as manageable as possible.

What to Say — Welcome Back

You: I’m really glad to have you back — and there’s no pressure to be at full speed. I’ve arranged [specific adjustments]. Check in with me at the end of today, and please tell me if anything feels like too much.

Call HR

Contact HR before making any formal leave arrangements or workload adjustments.

09

Return After Serious Medical Leave or Injury

Illness, surgery, significant injury, or extended health-related absence.

Guiding Principle 6

Care before process. The return conversation is about the person — not the paperwork.

Full protocol: Appendix B of the Wellness Plan.

Before the Return

  • Confirm the anticipated return date with the staff member
  • Ensure required medical clearance or documentation is received — coordinate with HR
  • Review their role and plan the transition from substitute or coverage
  • Identify any known limitations or accommodations to consider with HR
  • Prepare a summary of key updates from while they were out

The Return Conversation

Schedule a warm, unhurried check-in on or near the return day. This is a care conversation — not a performance review.

  • Welcome the staff member back genuinely — no pressure to be at full speed
  • Walk through current responsibilities and any upcoming deadlines
  • Clarify any temporary adjustments in place
  • Ask: ‘Is there anything I didn’t think of that would help?’
  • Set a follow-up check-in for two weeks out

Short-Term Adjustments

  • Prioritize essential responsibilities; hold non-critical tasks for a defined period
  • Flexibility with non-critical deadlines
  • Accommodate time for ongoing appointments or physical needs
  • Additional check-ins during the transition period

Temporary vs. permanent: Informal short-term adjustments are fine. Anything ongoing requires a formal ADA accommodation process through HR — coordinate before agreeing to it.

What to Say — Return Conversation

You: Welcome back — we’re really glad you’re here. Let’s walk through the next couple of weeks. I’ve arranged [describe adjustments]. Is there anything else that would help?

Call HR

Coordinate with HR on FMLA, ADA, and all return-to-work compliance. Document all accommodations.

10

Pregnancy Accommodations

Supporting pregnant staff under the PWFA and ADA.

Guiding Principle 6

Care before process. Requesting an accommodation is never a liability.

Full protocol: Appendix C of the Wellness Plan.

When a Request Is Made

  • Receive the request with care and without visible judgment
  • Contact HR immediately — do not commit to specific accommodations before consulting HR
  • Engage in the interactive process together with HR and the staff member
  • Document the agreed plan in writing with HR
  • Review and adjust the plan as the pregnancy progresses

Common Accommodations Under PWFA / ADA

  • Additional restroom, water, or rest breaks
  • Ability to sit intermittently while working
  • Temporary modification of lifting or physically demanding tasks
  • Schedule flexibility for prenatal appointments
  • Adjusted duty assignments and supervision requirements
Evaluation During Accommodation — Non-Negotiable

No staff member is rated negatively for: requesting or using an approved accommodation, having duties temporarily adjusted, or using protected leave. Evaluate on the responsibilities that remained assigned during the accommodation period.

What to Say — When a Staff Member Raises Pregnancy-Related Needs

You: Thank you for telling me. Let’s get HR involved so we work through this the right way. In the meantime — what’s been feeling hardest right now?

Call HR

All pregnancy accommodations must be coordinated through and documented by HR. No informal agreements.

11

Parental Leave Reintegration

Returning after maternity, paternity, or adoption leave.

Guiding Principle 6

Welcoming a child is one of life’s biggest transitions. We make the return feel supported, not jarring.

Full protocol: Appendix D of the Wellness Plan.

Before the Return

  • Confirm return date and coordinate transition from long-term substitute or coverage
  • Review key upcoming responsibilities and deadlines
  • Complete required HR documentation
  • Communicate relevant updates that occurred during the absence

The Reintegration Conversation

  • Warm welcome — no pressure to be at full speed immediately
  • Walk through upcoming responsibilities and any changes during their absence
  • Confirm any short-term support in place
  • Ask: ‘What would help most in your first couple of weeks back?’
  • Schedule a two-week follow-up check-in

Workplace Requirements — Coordinate With HR

  • Lactation accommodations — time and a private space are required under the PUMP Act
  • Schedule flexibility for childcare transitions in early weeks
  • All legally required accommodations documented with HR before the return date
What to Say — Return Conversation

You: Welcome back — really good to have you here. Let’s do a quick walk-through of what’s coming up. You don’t need to hit the ground running today. Is there anything that would make the first couple of weeks easier?

Call HR

Confirm PUMP Act lactation accommodations with HR before the return date — not after they return and ask.

12

Family Crisis & Caregiver Stress

Divorce, custody, serious family illness, and ongoing caregiving demands.

Guiding Principle 6

When life outside school becomes overwhelming, we meet people with support — not just policy.

Full protocol: Appendix E of the Wellness Plan.

When You Become Aware of a Situation

  • Initiate a private check-in — don’t wait for them to come to you
  • Express support without requiring disclosure of personal details
  • Listen to understand any immediate work impact — don’t probe personal specifics
  • Reinforce that support is available while expectations remain clear

Short-Term Support You Can Offer

  • Temporary flexibility with non-critical deadlines
  • Prioritization of essential responsibilities
  • Adjusted meeting expectations when possible
  • Additional check-ins for support and clarity
  • Leave planning and proactive coverage coordination

All adjustments must be temporary, clearly communicated, and regularly reviewed.

For Ongoing Situations

  • Schedule periodic check-ins — supportive, not intrusive
  • Monitor workload and adjust support within operational limits
  • Ensure expectations remain consistent and clearly communicated
  • Align with other leaders on the level of support being provided
What to Say — Opening Check-In

You: I wanted to check in and see how you’re doing. You don’t need to share anything you’re not comfortable with — I just want you to know I’m here. If there’s anything that would make work more manageable right now, I want to know.

Staff: Things have been complicated at home.

You: You don’t need to tell me more than that. Let’s talk about work — what parts feel hardest to manage right now?

Privacy Is Non-Negotiable

What staff share in these conversations stays with you and HR only. Do not share personal details with colleagues.

13

Mental Health Support

Everyday culture, available resources, and crisis response.

Guiding Principle 7

We treat mental health with the same respect as physical health.

Full protocol: Appendix G of the Wellness Plan.

Resources — Know These and Share Them Proactively

ResourceAccess
Employee Assistance Program (EAP)Free, confidential, 3–8 sessions per issue. Covers staff and household members. Contact HR for current provider and access info.
988 Suicide & Crisis LifelineCall or text 988 — available 24/7
Crisis Text LineText HOME to 741741
Community mental health referralsRequest from HR at any time — no explanation required

Your Everyday Role in Mental Health Culture

  • Speak about mental health directly and without stigma language
  • Mention EAP naturally during check-ins — normalize it before anyone urgently needs it
  • Never respond to a mental health disclosure with inconvenience, alarm, or visible distance
  • Do not treat a staff member differently after a mental health disclosure

Mental Health Leave

  • Staff are not required to disclose a diagnosis beyond what their leave type legally requires
  • Handle exactly as any serious medical leave — same structure, same confidentiality, same care
  • Mental health leave will not affect evaluation, scheduling, or any professional decision

Acute Crisis — Step by Step

  1. Stay calm. Remain present with the staff member.
  2. Listen without minimizing or rushing to problem-solve
  3. Ask directly: ‘Are you thinking about hurting yourself?’ — this is safe and important to ask
  4. Contact HR immediately; call 911 if there is immediate safety risk
  5. Arrange coverage — do not let operational pressure trap the staff member
  6. Provide 988 Lifeline and Crisis Text Line information
  7. Follow up privately within 24 hours; log and submit to HR
What to Say — Normalizing Support During a Routine Check-In

You: The EAP is available to you — completely confidential, free, and no judgment attached. I mention it not because I think something is wrong, but because I want you to know it’s there before you might need it.

What to Say — Crisis Moment

You: I’m right here with you. You are not in trouble. I just want to make sure you’re safe. Can you tell me what’s going on?

Call HR

Contact HR immediately in any mental health crisis. You do not manage this alone.

14

Traumatic School Event Response

Death, safety incident, community violence, or significant on-campus emergency.

Guiding Principle 6

Care before process. Safety and alignment first — then communication, then support.

Full protocol: Appendix F of the Wellness Plan.

First 24 Hours — In Order

  1. Confirm physical safety of all students and staff
  2. Activate emergency protocols; contact network leadership
  3. Convene leadership team — align on roles and messaging before anyone communicates publicly
  4. Draft communication: factual, calm, acknowledges emotional impact, no speculation
  5. Communicate with staff before information spreads informally
  6. Identify directly affected staff; schedule individual check-ins
  7. Coordinate access to on-site counselors or EAP crisis services
  8. Schedule 24-hour leadership debrief to assess ongoing needs
  9. Document actions taken and submit initial report to HR

What Your Communication Must Do

  • Be factual — no speculation or unverified details
  • Acknowledge the emotional impact directly and without minimizing
  • Be calm — your tone sets the tone for the school
  • Clarify immediate expectations and what happens next
What to Say to Staff

You: We recognize this situation may be emotionally difficult for many of you. Our focus right now is supporting students and staff while maintaining safety, communication, and care for one another. Here is what we know: [facts only]. I will update you again by [specific time].

Recovery Timeline

TimeframeLeader Actions
Within 24–72 hoursStaff check-ins; clarify supports and expectations; document
Within 1–2 weeksContinue monitoring well-being; adjust workload if needed; reassess climate
Within 2–6 weeksLeadership debrief; lessons learned; support for delayed trauma responses

Secondary trauma: Counselors, social workers, and teachers supporting grieving students are most at risk. Rotate crisis responsibilities when possible. Monitor these staff members actively in the weeks following the event.

Call HR

Contact HR within the first hour — before communicating with families or the community.

15

Staff Recognition & Culture

Simple, consistent, meaningful — every single month.

Guiding Principle 8

We recognize people — consistently, genuinely, and simply.

Every Week — Non-Negotiable

  • 2–3 personal, specific appreciation messages — name what the person actually did
  • At least one public acknowledgment in a staff meeting or group communication
  • A shout-out or highlight of specific staff work or effort

Monthly Culture Rhythm

MonthFocus & Examples
Jul / AugWelcome & connection — staff breakfast, team activity, leadership vision-setting, welcome tokens
SeptemberRelationship building — optional team lunch or coffee; shout-out board; weekly recognition in meetings
OctoberAppreciation & momentum — ‘caught you doing great’ notes; small recognition moments in staff meetings
NovemberGratitude — gratitude wall; appreciation breakfast before break; leadership thank-you messages to everyone
DecemberSemester closure — simple optional gathering; recognition of team accomplishments; reflection activity
JanuaryRe-energize — welcome back message; coffee or snack bar during first week; optional goal-setting
FebruaryMid-year appreciation — appreciation week; notes from leadership; optional peer recognition activity
MarchHigh-stress support — snacks, short appreciation messages; fewer events, more presence and check-ins
AprilSustaining energy — small recognition moments; optional treat day; continued shout-outs

What Good Recognition Looks Like

  • Specific — names exactly what the person did, not a generic ‘great job’
  • Timely — happens close to when the effort occurred
  • Genuine — reflects something you actually noticed, not a script
  • Varied — balance public and private recognition; individual and team
What to Say

You: I want to specifically call out what you did with [student / situation] last week. That was not easy, and the way you handled it makes this school stronger. Thank you.

Recognition is a leadership performance expectation — not a personality preference. Consistently failing to acknowledge staff contributions is a leadership gap and a coaching matter.

Self-Assessment & Reflection

Weekly reflection — scoring, interpreting gaps, and acting on results.

Guiding Principle 10

We hold ourselves accountable — not to perfection, but to honest reflection.

Complete weekly or bi-weekly. The goal is honest reflection, not performance. The full assessment tool is in Appendix I of the Wellness Plan.

Quick Contacts, Rules & Deadlines

HR triggers, never-do list, annual calendar, and crisis lines.

Always Call HR First When

Any accommodation request — pregnancy, medical, disability related; FMLA, ADA, or leave compliance questions of any kind; a mental health crisis or safety concern arises; Grief Support Protocol is being activated; a traumatic school event response is underway; a staff member files a complaint or raises a concern about retaliation; you are unsure whether a situation requires formal documentation.

Never Do These Things

  • Make staff feel ashamed or judged for requesting leave or accommodations
  • Make promises in stay conversations that you cannot keep
  • Treat mental health leave differently from any other medical leave
  • Share personal staff details beyond what is operationally necessary
  • Launch a major initiative in a high-stress period without leadership team approval
  • Respond to staff feedback with defensiveness or visible distancing behavior
  • Make informal accommodation agreements without HR documentation
  • Skip weekly check-ins because things seem fine
