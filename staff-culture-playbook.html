About This Playbook

Why it exists, and how it is used across the year.

Why It Exists

This Playbook makes our shared cultural commitments visible, names the behaviors we expect and protect, and creates common language for how we work together. Strong schools are built on strong adult culture — how we work together is as important as what we do.

This Playbook is:

  • A shared set of commitments and behavioral norms
  • A guide for communication, collaboration, and accountability
  • A living document — referenced, discussed, and practiced daily

This Playbook does not replace the Employee Handbook, the Leadership Handbook, board-approved policies, or legal/compliance requirements. It complements them by answering: “What does it look like to live our values together, every day?”

Why Staff Culture Matters

Matchbook Learning serves students and families who rely on us to be consistent, reliable, collaborative, emotionally grounded, and mission-driven. When adult culture is healthy, students experience stability, families experience trust, staff experience belonging and growth, and leaders can focus on improvement rather than repair.

When adult culture breaks down, students feel it first. Staff culture is not “extra” — it is foundational to student success, staff retention, and organizational sustainability.

Shared Ownership

Culture at MBLI is not owned by HR, leadership, or any single department. It is co-created by every teacher, support staff member, leader, and network team. Every person contributes through how they communicate, respond to feedback, support colleagues, handle conflict, and uphold shared expectations.

How This Playbook Is Used

WhenHow It Is Used
OnboardingAll new leaders and staff review key commitments, communication expectations, and cultural norms as part of onboarding.
Annual ReviewRevisited at leadership retreats, PD sessions, or beginning-of-year meetings to reinforce expectations and identify growth areas.
Coaching & FeedbackReferenced in coaching conversations, performance discussions, and conflict resolution. It is a mirror — not a weapon.
Moments of TensionUsed to ground conversations during disagreement, change, or stress. Provides shared language before reactions.

This Playbook is revised intentionally when needed. Revisions are communicated clearly and will be reflected in updated onboarding and coaching materials.

Our Cultural North Star

Our staff culture mission, vision, and six core values.

Staff Culture Mission & Vision

Vision

A staff culture where adults trust one another, feedback is normal and growth-oriented, challenges are addressed directly rather than personalized, leadership is visible and fair, and people want to stay, grow, and lead.

Mission

To build and protect a professional, inclusive, and accountable adult culture where people feel respected, supported, and challenged — so students can thrive.

Core Values

These six values define how we show up as adults at MBLI — in decisions, interactions, and especially under stress.

1. Students First, Always

Every decision we make is grounded in what is best for students — academically, emotionally, and developmentally.

Looks LikeDoes Not Look Like
Making decisions based on student impact, even when inconvenientMaking decisions based on adult comfort or convenience
Addressing adult issues quickly so they do not affect studentsLetting unresolved adult conflict spill into classrooms
Aligning resources to student needsProtecting systems or traditions that no longer serve students

2. Professionalism with Care

We hold high standards while treating people with dignity, empathy, and respect.

Looks LikeDoes Not Look Like
Giving clear, respectful feedback — even when it is hardAvoiding tough conversations to keep the peace
Addressing issues directly and privatelyVenting, gossiping, or correcting publicly
Assuming positive intent while holding expectationsBeing dismissive, sarcastic, or emotionally reactive

3. Ownership and Accountability

We own our roles, our decisions, and our impact. Accountability is how we build trust.

Looks LikeDoes Not Look Like
Following through on commitmentsBlaming others or circumstances
Addressing problems earlyHoping issues resolve themselves
Saying “That’s on me — here’s how I’ll fix it”Deflecting responsibility or moving the goalposts

4. Clarity Over Comfort

Clear expectations and honest communication are kinder — and more effective — than ambiguity.

Looks LikeDoes Not Look Like
Being transparent about decisions and expectationsHinting, implying, or being vague
Naming issues directly and respectfullyLetting confusion linger to avoid discomfort
Explaining the “why” behind decisionsSaying “because that’s what we’re doing”

5. Growth Mindset for Adults

We believe adults — like students — are capable of learning, improving, and growing.

Looks LikeDoes Not Look Like
Treating feedback as a tool, not a punishmentTaking feedback personally or defensively
Coaching before escalatingJumping straight to discipline without support
Reflecting and adjusting practiceDigging in or shutting down when challenged

6. Collective Responsibility

We succeed or struggle together. Culture is not someone else’s job.

Looks LikeDoes Not Look Like
Supporting colleagues during high-stress momentsSaying “That’s not my problem”
Reinforcing shared expectations consistentlyApplying standards unevenly
Speaking up when culture is misalignedStaying silent to avoid involvement
Culture is shaped by what leaders reward, tolerate, and correct. A simple question leaders should ask often: “Does this action move us closer to our Cultural North Star — or further away?”

The Leadership Mindset

How leaders show up, especially under stress.

The Leadership Mindset defines how leaders and staff show up at MBLI — especially during moments of stress, disagreement, or challenge. Leadership is not defined by title alone. It is defined by behavior, presence, and consistency. Leaders carry heightened responsibility for modeling this mindset and naming misalignment when it occurs.

How Leaders Show Up

MindsetLooks LikeDoes Not Look Like
Assume Positive IntentAsking clarifying questions before reacting; separating intent from impactJumping to conclusions; assigning motives without evidence; treating mistakes as character flaws
Student-Centered and Adult-SupportiveDecisions grounded in student needs; providing clarity and tools to staffPitting student needs against staff well-being; avoiding accountability “to be nice”
Calm, Consistent, Solution-OrientedResponding — not reacting; applying expectations fairly and predictablyLeading from frustration; changing expectations based on mood; dwelling on problems without action
Model AccountabilityOwning mistakes openly; following through; holding others accountable with clarityDeflecting responsibility; holding staff to standards leaders don’t meet themselves
Growth MindsetSeeking feedback; reflecting on practice; adjusting when something isn’t workingBeing defensive about feedback; treating development as optional
Emotional RegulationPausing before responding; keeping tone professional under stressYelling, sarcasm, or outbursts; making decisions in anger; using authority to intimidate

This mindset matters most during conflict, when performance is slipping, under external pressure, and when emotions are high. In these moments, leaders are expected to slow down, ground themselves, and return to these principles.

Trust, Psychological Safety & Belonging

What trust looks like, and the standards that protect it.

Strong adult culture is built on trust, psychological safety, and belonging. When adults feel respected, heard, supported, and held to clear standards, they perform at higher levels — and students benefit. Trust is not assumed here. It is built intentionally, consistently, and together.

What Trust Looks Like at MBLI

Trust Is Built WhenTrust Erodes When There Is
Words and actions alignInconsistency between what is said and done
Expectations are clear, consistent, and communicated in advanceFavoritism or uneven enforcement
Leaders follow through on commitmentsPublic correction or embarrassment
Feedback is direct, respectful, and privateWithheld expectations or “gotcha” leadership
Decisions are explained, not hiddenSurprise evaluations with no prior conversation

Psychological Safety

Psychological safety means staff can ask questions without embarrassment, admit mistakes without humiliation, offer ideas or respectful dissent, and receive feedback privately and professionally.

Psychological safety does NOT mean avoiding accountability, eliminating high standards, or agreeing with everything. We can hold high expectations and create safety at the same time.

Belonging

Belonging means every staff member feels seen and respected, has a voice in conversations that affect their work, is treated equitably, and knows their contributions matter. Belonging does not require uniformity — it requires respect and inclusion. It is mutual: it requires both inclusion and contribution.

Leadership Commitments for Building Trust

  • Listen First — Ask clarifying questions; seek context before reacting; avoid dismissal or interruption
  • Honor Voice Without Sacrificing Clarity — Invite input when appropriate; explain the “why” behind decisions; distinguish between consultation and consensus. Voice does not mean veto power.
  • Follow Through — Do what you say; close feedback loops; communicate promptly when plans change. Failure to follow through quickly undermines trust.
  • Practice Transparency — Communicate decisions clearly; share relevant context when possible; acknowledge when information cannot yet be shared

Non-Negotiable Standards

StandardWhat It Means
No Public ShamingFeedback is delivered privately and professionally. Mistakes are addressed with dignity.
No Surprise FeedbackExpectations are clear in advance; concerns are addressed early; evaluations reflect conversations already held.
No “Gotcha” LeadershipConcerns are not withheld; ambiguity is not weaponized; accountability is timely and fair.
Trust is most tested during change, performance concerns, conflict, and high-stress moments. In these moments: slow down, communicate clearly, avoid emotional escalation, and protect dignity. Culture is revealed under pressure.
05

Conflict, Repair & Reset

How we address harm and rebuild trust after it.

Conflict is a natural part of professional work. When adults work closely, under pressure, and in service of students, tension will occur. What defines our culture is not the absence of conflict — but how we handle it.

Unresolved tension erodes culture over time. Repair is not weakness — it is leadership.

Cultural Expectations

  • Address concerns directly with the person involved — speak to people, not about them
  • Avoid gossip or side conversations; raise concerns privately and promptly
  • Resolve conflict as soon as reasonably possible — delaying increases misunderstanding and risk
  • Maintain a solution-oriented mindset throughout; repair is about moving forward, not relitigating the past

The Repair Framework

When communication or behavior causes harm — intentional or not — repair is expected. Follow this five-step structure:

StepWhat to Do
1. Pause & ReflectBefore initiating repair, ask: What happened? What was my intent? What was the impact? What responsibility do I own? Self-awareness comes before conversation.
2. InitiateRepair conversations must be timely, private, direct, and calm. Example: “I want to revisit our conversation because I care about clarity and trust.”
3. Acknowledge ImpactEven positive intent can create negative impact. Name what may have felt unclear or misaligned. Avoid defensiveness. Own responsibility where appropriate. Owning impact builds credibility.
4. Clarify ForwardRepair does not eliminate accountability. Restate expectations clearly, align on next steps, and confirm mutual understanding. Trust grows when clarity follows repair.
5. Follow ThroughRepair without behavior change is ineffective. Honor commitments, monitor follow-through, and avoid repeating missteps. Consistency restores trust.

Repair does NOT mean reversing necessary decisions, lowering standards, avoiding accountability, or agreeing with everything. Repair strengthens relationships while preserving expectations.

Leader Responsibility in Conflict

When conflict escalates or persists, leaders are responsible for mediating fairly, ensuring all parties are heard, reinforcing professional standards, resetting expectations clearly, protecting dignity, and documenting when appropriate. Leaders model emotional regulation and fairness in moments of tension.

When Repair Is Not Enough

If conflict patterns persist, HR or a supervisor may be involved, issues may move into formal support or accountability processes, and documentation may be required. Repeated breakdowns must be addressed structurally — sincere repair is expected, but not a substitute for formal action when needed.

06

Equity, Consistency & Fairness

Applying expectations consistently and interrupting bias.

Culture is not defined by intention alone — it is defined by consistency. Equity, consistency, and fairness are foundational to trust, retention, and professional excellence.

Culture is what happens when no one is watching.

Applying Expectations Consistently

Consistency means the same expectations apply across similar roles, policies are enforced uniformly, accountability is not personality-based, and standards do not shift based on preference, pressure, or relationships. Consistency does not mean rigidity — it means predictability.

Inconsistent enforcement erodes trust faster than strict enforcement. Staff should never have to guess who will be held accountable, which behaviors matter, or whether rules apply evenly.

Equity vs. Equality

Equity means recognizing that different individuals may require different supports to meet shared expectations. At MBLI, standards are shared, supports may vary, and accountability remains consistent. Honoring individual circumstances does not mean lowering expectations — it means providing the appropriate path to meet them.

Interrupting Bias

Leaders are responsible for actively interrupting bias — both conscious and unconscious. This includes examining decision patterns, reflecting on discipline trends, ensuring feedback is based on behavior rather than personality, questioning assumptions before acting, and seeking input when unsure.

Bias can appear as:

  • Tone policing or labeling individuals rather than behaviors
  • Different tolerance levels for similar conduct across different staff members
  • Uneven growth or development opportunities
  • Informal exceptions not available to all

Avoiding Favoritism

Favoritism — whether real or perceived — damages culture. Leaders must avoid informal exceptions without clear rationale, selective enforcement of policies, private advantages not available to others, and over-reliance on personal relationships. Even perceived favoritism undermines morale. Transparency protects fairness.

Honoring Differences While Holding Shared Standards

MBLI respects diverse backgrounds, experiences, and communication styles. We create space for multiple perspectives and value professional identity and lived experience. At the same time, shared professional standards are non-negotiable, respect is required across differences, and accountability applies equally. Inclusion does not eliminate expectations — it strengthens them.

Leader Responsibilities

  • Apply policies consistently across all staff
  • Document decisions clearly with factual rationale
  • Reflect regularly on patterns of enforcement
  • Seek HR partnership when fairness may be questioned
  • Model impartial decision-making at all times

When equity concerns are raised: Address them promptly, review objectively against documented standards, and escalate to HR if necessary. Transparency and documentation protect both staff and leadership.

Leadership Presence & Visibility

Where leaders show up, and why it matters.

Where leaders are — and how they show up — matters. Visibility communicates priorities, builds trust, reinforces expectations, and supports staff and students in real time.

Leadership presence is not about surveillance. It is about support, stability, and stewardship.

Required Visibility Windows

WindowWhy It Matters
ArrivalGreeting students and families, monitoring safety, supporting staff during high-volume moments. Arrival sets the emotional climate of the school.
TransitionsClass changes, lunch, dismissal, movement between spaces. Transitions are culture moments — presence reinforces expectations and enables real-time coaching.
Instructional WalkthroughsRegular walkthroughs reinforce strong practice, offer real-time coaching, address issues early, and celebrate wins.
High-Stress MomentsState testing, discipline escalations, staffing shortages, family concerns, major events. Leaders stabilize the environment through calm visibility and solution-oriented support.

Visibility as Support — Not Surveillance

Leadership presence should feel encouraging, supportive, grounded, and collaborative. It should not feel intimidating, reactive, or compliance-focused. Leaders walk classrooms and hallways to reinforce strong practice, offer real-time coaching, address issues early, and celebrate wins. Visibility builds trust when it communicates partnership.

Balancing Office Work with Instructional Presence

Leadership roles include necessary administrative responsibilities. However, paperwork cannot replace presence, emails cannot substitute for relationships, and data review cannot replace classroom visibility. Leaders must intentionally balance office-based tasks with instructional walkthroughs, informal staff check-ins, and student-facing engagement. Calendar time should reflect school priorities — not just administrative demands.

When Visibility Is Lacking

When leaders are consistently absent from key moments: expectations weaken, staff feel unsupported, student behavior may escalate, and culture becomes fragmented.

Important

Lack of visible leadership presence will be addressed as a performance issue.

Decision-Making & Change Management

How decisions are made and communicated through change.

How we make decisions and how we manage change directly impacts trust, morale, and performance. Change should feel purposeful — not chaotic. We do not tolerate drift or ambiguity. We commit to timely decisions and clear communication.

Transparency in Decision-Making

Transparency means explaining the “why” behind decisions when appropriate, clarifying what input was considered, distinguishing between consultation and final authority, and naming what is known and what is still evolving.

Transparency does NOT mean full consensus on every decision, sharing confidential information, or delaying action to achieve universal agreement. Clarity builds trust — even when outcomes are not universally preferred.

What Staff Can Expect

SituationStaff Can ExpectStaff Should Not Experience
All Decision ProcessesClear timelines, honest communication, space for appropriate feedback, consistent messagingMixed messages, public leadership disagreement, surprise changes without context
Transitions (Leadership, Role, Structural)Direct communication, clarified responsibilities, realigned expectations, support during adjustmentUncertainty ignored or dismissed; lack of clarity about new structures or responsibilities
New InitiativesClear goals, defined roles, training/support, realistic timelines, opportunities for feedbackConstant pivots without closure; initiative fatigue from poor sequencing or communication
Hard SeasonsCalm grounded leadership, frequent communication, reinforcement of shared priorities, dignity preservedErratic decision-making, inconsistent standards, silence or avoidance

Leader Responsibilities During Change

  • Align privately before communicating publicly — leadership disagreement is handled internally, not broadcast
  • Model emotional regulation at all times
  • Reinforce the mission and purpose behind the change
  • Do not undermine decisions through informal channels or passive resistance
  • Manage concerns through appropriate channels

Staff Responsibilities During Change

  • Engage in change professionally and raise concerns respectfully through appropriate channels
  • Seek clarification rather than assume or speculate
  • Avoid rumor cycles — go to the source
  • Support implementation once decisions are finalized, even when input was not adopted
Professional culture requires both voice and alignment. Once a decision is made, staff are expected to implement it professionally regardless of personal preference.

Leadership Commitments

What we will do, and what we will not.

Leadership at MBLI is defined by behavior, not title. These commitments are non-negotiable. They guide how we lead, communicate, and hold responsibility for culture. Failure to uphold these commitments will be addressed through coaching, performance management, or corrective action.

We Will…

CommitmentWhat It Means in Practice
Put students first while supporting adults fullyMake decisions grounded in student outcomes and provide the clarity and support staff need to succeed.
Communicate clearly, directly, and respectfullyExplain expectations, decisions, and changes with professionalism and care. No hinting or implying.
Address issues early and privatelyNot allow small concerns to grow into larger problems. Feedback belongs in private, professional conversations.
Apply expectations consistently and fairlyAvoid favoritism — real or perceived. Uphold standards across roles, relationships, and circumstances.
Listen before reactingSeek understanding, invite perspective, and remain open to feedback before drawing conclusions.
Follow through on commitmentsDo what we say we will do — or communicate promptly and honestly when circumstances change.
Model accountability and growthOwn mistakes, adjust when needed, and demonstrate the behaviors we expect from others.
Remain calm and solution-oriented under pressureCulture is revealed most clearly during difficult moments. Emotional regulation is a leadership requirement.

We Do Not…

Prohibited BehaviorWhy It Matters
Publicly shame, embarrass, or correct staffFeedback belongs in private, professional conversations — always.
Surprise staff with feedback or evaluationsExpectations and concerns are communicated in advance. Evaluations reflect conversations already held.
Gossip, vent downward, or undermine decisionsWe manage up and across — not down. Leadership disagreement is addressed privately.
Weaponize ambiguity or engage in “gotcha” leadershipWe are clear, fair, and transparent. Ambiguity is not a management tool.
Avoid difficult conversationsAvoidance erodes trust. Clarity builds it. Leaders address concerns directly and promptly.
Allow inconsistency to weaken cultureStandards apply regardless of role, relationship, or circumstance. No exceptions without documented rationale.

Leadership is not about authority. Leadership is about responsibility. These commitments exist because culture is built through consistent action — not position. They are the promises we make to each other and to the students we serve.
