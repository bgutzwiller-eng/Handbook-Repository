Staff Wellness & Stability Plan

Guiding Principles for School Leaders · 2026–2027

"These are not rules to enforce. They are intentions to return to."

A Note on How to Use This Document

What we are trying to be — and a way to return to it.

A policy document tells us what we must do. A guiding principles document tells us what we are trying to be — and gives us a way to return to that when the year gets hard, when we fall short, or when a situation doesn’t fit neatly into a checklist.

School leadership is demanding, season-dependent, and genuinely complex. There will be stretches of the year — testing, year-end, crises — when some of what this document describes cannot happen the way you’d want it to. That is understood. These principles are not a performance standard that resets to failure when a week goes sideways. They are a compass.

The question this document asks is simple: When we have the capacity to show up for our staff — and when we don’t — what are the values we’re trying to hold onto, and what does care look like at every point on that continuum?

This document is intended to support reflection and consistency in how we lead people through difficult and demanding work. It is not a policy manual, evaluation rubric, or guarantee of specific outcomes. School leadership remains complex and capacity-dependent, and these principles are intended to help us return to what matters most as leaders.

How to Read This Document

Each principle includes a core statement, what it looks like in practice during a normal season, and what it looks like when the season is hard and capacity is stretched. Read the whole document once. Then keep it somewhere you’ll actually reach for it.

This plan works alongside the Staff Culture Playbook and the HOS Quick-Reference Playbook. The Culture Playbook describes how staff work together. The Quick-Reference Playbook gives leaders practical tools for specific situations. This document describes the values underneath both — the why behind the how.

01

We believe staff wellness and student success are inseparable.

Why staff wellness is the foundation everything else stands on.

When staff feel respected, supported, and sustainable in their work, students benefit. Protecting staff well-being is not a distraction from the mission — it is how the mission is sustained.

This principle is the foundation underneath everything else in this document. It is why the other principles exist.

Schools can develop cultures where staff exhaustion becomes normalized — where working through illness, skipping lunch, and absorbing constant change become signs of dedication rather than warning signs. Matchbook Learning is committed to building a different kind of culture. Not one where expectations are lower, but one where the adults are not expected to sacrifice themselves indefinitely for the school to function.

This belief does not require perfect execution every week. It requires honest attention — noticing when people are struggling, when systems are creating unnecessary pressure, and when care has been absent for too long.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • Leadership decisions are evaluated not only by operational outcomes, but by their effect on the people doing the work
  • When something breaks down for a staff member — a loss, a health crisis, an overwhelming season — the first response is human, not procedural
  • When staff morale or retention trends in a concerning direction, the question leaders ask first is: what are we contributing to this?
  • Staff are not asked to prove their worth by overextending — sustainability is treated as a professional value, not a limitation
When the Year Is Hard and Capacity Is Stretched

This principle is most important to return to when things are hardest. It doesn’t require you to solve everything — it requires you not to forget that the people around you are carrying something real.

02

We lead through partnership — clear on direction, strong in support, and generous in trust.

Clear on direction, strong in support, and generous in trust.

Staff do their best work when they understand what matters most, have genuine latitude in how they pursue it, and are supported in getting there. At Matchbook Learning, coaching exists to strengthen professional judgment, not replace it. Clear expectations are what make real autonomy possible. Strong coaching is what makes it sustainable.

Matchbook Learning is a coaching school. That means leaders are invested in teacher growth — not as a compliance function, but as a genuine commitment to helping every educator get better at the work. That coaching relationship works best when it lives inside a culture of trust and professional respect, where staff have real latitude to make instructional decisions and leaders use their influence to support, not to script.

The distinction between support and control matters enormously in how school culture feels. A school where leaders set clear priorities and then trust staff to pursue them feels different from a school where leaders define both the outcome and every step toward it. The first builds investment and professional identity. The second builds compliance — and eventually, disengagement.

This principle asks leaders to be specific about what is non-negotiable and honest about what is flexible. When those lines are blurry, staff fill the gap with anxiety. When they are clear, staff can lead within them — and will often do so in ways that exceed what the leader imagined.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • Schoolwide priorities and instructional non-negotiables are identified at the start of the year and communicated clearly — staff know what matters most and where to direct their energy
  • Within those priorities, staff have real latitude in how they teach, organize their classrooms, build relationships, and approach their professional growth — professional judgment is not a workaround, it is the expectation
  • Coaching is a partnership, not surveillance. Leaders use observation and feedback to support teachers in reaching their own goals alongside school goals — not to enforce uniformity of method
  • When leaders make decisions that affect staff work, they explain the reasoning — not to justify themselves, but because understanding the why allows staff to align with it genuinely
  • Staff voice is sought before major decisions when there is time to incorporate it — and when there isn’t, the decision is explained after
  • Coaching conversations focus on student outcomes and professional growth — not on whether a teacher does things the way the leader would do them

What This Looks Like When the Year Is Hard

High-demand periods often create pressure toward control — leaders get anxious, start checking more, and tighten the reins on the professional judgment staff need to function well. This is worth noticing — because it’s exactly backward. The seasons when leaders most want to control are often the seasons when trust matters most.

Questions to Ask Yourself During High-Stress Periods

Am I coaching towards better outcomes — or am I managing my own anxiety by adding oversight? Are the additional check-ins I’m adding serving teachers and students — or serving my need to feel in control? When did I last tell a teacher they were doing good work and then get out of the way?

03

We protect people’s time as seriously as we protect their dignity.

Time is one of the most concrete expressions of respect.

Time is one of the most concrete expressions of respect. When we pile on initiatives, call unnecessary meetings, interrupt planning time, or expect responsiveness outside of work hours, we communicate — however unintentionally — that staff time is ours to spend.

Every leader understands that the school year is genuinely demanding. Some periods will require more from everyone. But there is a meaningful difference between seasons that are temporarily hard and a culture where overextension becomes the permanent baseline.

This principle asks leaders to be intentional stewards of staff time — to hold a higher bar before adding responsibilities, to be honest about what is actually essential, and to protect the margins that allow people to recover between demands.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • Before adding any new responsibility or initiative, leaders consider what can be reduced or removed to make space for it
  • Meetings have clear purposes and end on time — and when something could be an email, it is an email
  • Planning time is treated as protected — not a reserve of available time for other things to fill
  • Communication after hours is reserved for genuine urgency, and staff know they are not expected to respond otherwise
  • High-demand periods on the calendar are anticipated and managed — not landed on staff as surprises

What This Looks Like When the Year Is Hard

During intense seasons — testing, compliance periods, year-end — the temptation is to add more communication, more meetings, more oversight. The opposite is usually what’s needed.

During High-Stress Periods, This Principle Looks Like

Canceling or shortening meetings that can wait. Naming explicitly to staff that a hard season is temporary and that you see how much they’re carrying. Resisting the urge to launch new things until the pressure has passed. Making one fewer demand rather than one more, whenever you have a choice.

04

We earn trust through consistency, not intention.

What leaders do, day after day, is what builds trust.

Staff trust is built through what leaders actually do, day after day — not through what leaders say they value or intend. Consistency in communication, fairness in decisions, and follow-through on commitments are what create an environment where people feel safe.

Many leaders have good intentions and poor follow-through. The gap between the two is where trust erodes. A leader who says they value staff voice but never responds to feedback, who promises clarity but delivers shifting priorities, who espouses fairness but applies expectations inconsistently — that leader will lose trust regardless of their intentions.

This principle is a commitment to the harder work: not just caring about staff well-being, but doing the things that actually communicate care consistently, even when it is inconvenient or imperfect.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • When leaders say they will follow up on something, they do — or they explain what prevented it
  • Expectations are applied consistently across staff, and when exceptions are made, they are explained
  • Staff feedback is not only collected but acted on — and when it isn’t, staff hear why
  • Communication is not reactive — leaders keep staff informed about things that affect their work before those things happen
  • Leadership teams align with each other before communicating significant changes, so staff receive consistent guidance from all directions

What This Looks Like When the Year Is Hard

Busy seasons are when follow-through most often slips. This is understandable — and it’s also the moment when small gestures of consistency matter most.

When You Can’t Do Everything

Do what you said you would, even if it is smaller or later than intended. Acknowledge the gap when you can’t — ‘I said I’d get back to you about X and I haven’t. Here’s where things stand.’ Naming a gap honestly is itself an act of consistency. It tells staff you are paying attention.

05

We make it safe to tell the truth.

Psychological safety is the presence of trust, not the absence of accountability.

Staff should be able to raise concerns, ask hard questions, and respectfully disagree without fear of consequences. Psychological safety is not the absence of accountability — it is the presence of enough trust that honest conversation can happen even when it is uncomfortable.

The quality of a school’s culture is often visible in who speaks up and who stays quiet. When staff consistently hold back — in meetings, in check-ins, in surveys — it is usually not because everything is fine. It is because the cost of honesty feels too high.

Building psychological safety is a leadership behavior, not a policy. It happens through how leaders respond when staff raise difficult things. A leader who becomes visibly defensive, who cools toward a staff member after receiving feedback, who conflates professional disagreement with disloyalty — that leader is actively eroding safety, however unintentionally.

This principle requires leaders to be honest about how their reactions land — even when the staff member is wrong, even when the feedback is uncomfortable, even when the timing is terrible.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • When staff raise a concern, the first response is curiosity, not defense — ‘Tell me more’ before ‘Here’s why that’s not accurate’
  • Staff who speak up, ask questions, or respectfully disagree do not experience any change in how they are treated
  • When staff feedback changes something, leaders name the connection — staff should know their voice had effect
  • When feedback doesn’t change something, leaders explain why — staff should know their voice was heard even when the outcome was different
  • Multiple channels exist for raising concerns, because not everyone can speak directly to their supervisor
When the Year Is Hard and You’re Running on Empty

The temptation is to become less open — to manage the input because you don’t have the bandwidth for it. But this is exactly when staff most need to know it’s safe to tell you what’s happening. Psychological safety at low capacity looks like: ‘I don’t have the bandwidth to solve this today, but I want to hear it and I will come back to it.’ That sentence is enough to keep the door open.

06

We respond to struggle with care before we respond with process.

Process and logistics have their place — and they follow, not lead.

When a staff member is experiencing grief, health challenges, family crisis, mental health strain, or the cumulative weight of a difficult year, the first response is human. Process, documentation, and operational logistics all have their place — and they follow, not lead.

Schools are full of specific protocols for specific situations — leave policies, accommodation procedures, return-to-work processes. These exist for good reasons. But no protocol replaces the experience of being seen by another person before the paperwork begins.

This principle is about sequence. It does not ask leaders to ignore operational needs or to promise what they cannot deliver. It asks them to lead with their humanity — to make contact before they make plans, to listen before they problem-solve, to express care before they explain procedure.

It also asks leaders to be honest that they cannot always provide the same level of support throughout the year. What care looks like in a lighter month and what it looks like during testing week are different things. The principle holds in both — but its expression will vary.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • When a leader becomes aware that a staff member is going through something significant, they reach out personally — not through email, not by waiting for the staff member to come to them
  • The opening of that conversation is relational, not operational: ‘How are you doing?’ before ‘What do we need to arrange?’
  • Staff are never made to feel like a burden or a logistical problem, even when their absence creates real operational challenges
  • When operational needs must be addressed, leaders are honest about them — and still lead with care first
  • Staff know what support resources are available before they need them, not only after a crisis is underway

Support doesn’t have to be comprehensive to be meaningful. A two-minute check-in — ‘I’ve been thinking about you. How are you doing?’ — is not nothing. It is often enough to sustain a person through a hard stretch while you figure out what else is possible. The principle is that care comes first. Not that care must be elaborate, extended, or perfectly executed. The minimum is presence. Start there.

07

We treat mental health with the same respect as physical health.

Supporting mental health is a leadership responsibility, not a perk.

Mental and emotional well-being are not optional dimensions of staff health. They are fundamental to whether people can sustain the work of teaching and supporting students over time. Supporting them is not a wellness perk — it is a leadership responsibility.

The culture of many schools — and many organizations — still treats mental health as secondary: something private, something personal, something outside the scope of workplace leadership. Matchbook Learning is committed to a different stance.

This means leaders speak about mental health openly and without stigma. It means staff are not made to feel that accessing support is a sign of weakness or professional inadequacy. It means mental health leave is handled with exactly the same care and confidentiality as any other medical leave. And it means the systems leaders create — the workload expectations, the communication norms, the pace of change — are evaluated in part by whether they contribute to or undermine the emotional sustainability of the people doing the work.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • The EAP and mental health resources are communicated proactively at the start of each year — and after any significant organizational stressor — not only when someone appears to be in crisis
  • Leaders normalize help-seeking by speaking about stress and support without euphemism or minimization
  • A staff member who takes mental health leave returns to the same professional standing, relationships, and evaluation process as any other medical leave
  • When signs of burnout or emotional exhaustion appear, leaders respond with support — not surveillance

Burnout — An Organizational Concern, Not a Personal Failure

Burnout is what happens when sustained demands consistently exceed available support, recovery time, and resources. It is not a character flaw or a sign that someone is poorly suited to the work. When it appears across a team or role, it is almost always a signal about systems, not individuals.

Leaders are responsible for monitoring burnout indicators — not to evaluate staff, but to identify where the organization may be contributing to an unsustainable environment. Changes in energy, engagement, work patterns, and attendance are worth noticing and worth asking about.

When the Year Is Hard — Caring for Yourself, Too

School leaders experience burnout as well. This principle applies to you. You cannot consistently model sustainable leadership while privately running on empty. Naming your own capacity limits to your supervisor, using your own leave, and accessing support when you need it are not signs of weakness in a leader — they are signs of the kind of self-awareness this organization is trying to build.

08

We recognize people — consistently, genuinely, and simply.

Being seen and appreciated is a basic human need.

Being seen and appreciated for the work you do is a basic human need. It does not require elaborate events or formal programs. It requires leaders who pay attention and who say so.

Recognition that is rare, generic, or obviously performative does little. Recognition that is specific, timely, and genuine — that names what someone actually did and why it mattered — lands differently. It tells a person that someone noticed. And in the relentlessness of a school year, being noticed is not a small thing.

This principle asks leaders to make appreciation a habit, not an event. Not a monthly all-staff shout-out, but the walk down the hall to say ‘I saw what you did with that student yesterday and I want you to know it mattered.’ Not the end-of-year speech, but the Tuesday morning message that takes ninety seconds to write.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • 2–3 personal, specific appreciation messages each week — written or spoken, individual or small group
  • Recognition in staff meetings that names the specific contribution, not just the general effort
  • Milestones acknowledged — years of service, professional achievements, a hard week navigated with grace
  • End-of-semester and end-of-year moments that reflect on shared accomplishment, not just individual performance
  • A culture where staff also recognize each other — which starts with leaders modeling it

A loose rhythm throughout the year keeps recognition from being seasonal or reactive. These are suggestions, not mandates — adapt them to what your school community responds to.

When the Year Is Hard

Recognition is often the first thing that disappears during demanding periods. And it’s one of the things that most helps people keep going. Even during testing week, even during the sprint to the end of the year — a specific, genuine ‘thank you’ takes thirty seconds and costs nothing.

09

We stay curious about our people — and use what we learn.

Stay conversations and check-ins keep leaders connected to reality.

The leaders who retain and sustain their staff are the ones who actually know how their people are doing — not by assumption, but by asking. Stay conversations, check-ins, and climate data are not administrative tasks. They are how leaders stay connected to the reality their staff are living.

It is easy to assume. Leaders who see a staff member functioning normally tend to assume they are fine. Leaders who are busy tend to assume things are going well unless someone tells them otherwise. But staff often do not tell unless they believe it will help — and that belief depends on whether anything has happened when they’ve spoken up before.

This principle asks leaders to make curiosity a practice: to ask, to listen without pre-deciding, to use what they hear, and to close the loop so that speaking up has visible effect.

What This Looks Like in Practice

  • Stay interviews are conducted twice a year — not as a compliance task, but as a genuine listening conversation — with the goal of understanding what is sustaining each person and what is making their work harder than it needs to be
  • Check-ins with individual staff happen regularly throughout the year, not only when concerns are visible
  • Climate surveys are administered and the results are shared with staff — including what leadership is doing in response
  • When a staff member shares something difficult, the leader follows up — with action, or with an honest explanation of why things didn’t change
  • Staff turnover and absenteeism trends are reviewed regularly as signals about organizational health, not just individual circumstances

Stay Conversations — What They Are and Aren’t

A stay conversation is not a performance conversation. It is an informal but intentional discussion about how a staff member is experiencing their work, what is sustaining them, and where they are struggling. It is a listening conversation — the leader’s job is to understand, not to explain or defend.

Useful questions to build from:

  • What aspects of your work are going well this year?
  • What feels most challenging right now?
  • Are there obstacles that are making your job harder than it needs to be?
  • What would make this a place where you want to stay and do your best work?
  • What’s one thing I could do differently that would help you?
When Full Stay Interviews Aren’t Possible

During demanding periods, a full 30-minute stay conversation may not be realistic. A scaled-down version is still worth something: a five-minute check-in in the hallway, a short ‘how are you really doing?’ before a meeting, a quick message that says ‘I’ve been thinking about how much you’re carrying — how can I help?’ The goal is not the format. The goal is that each person knows their leader is paying attention to them as a person, not just as a role.

10

We hold ourselves accountable — not to perfection, but to honest reflection.

A tool for honest reflection, not a performance metric.

These principles are aspirational. There will be weeks — sometimes long stretches — when the demands of the school year make it genuinely difficult to live up to all of them. That is expected and understood. What matters is that leaders remain honest about the gap and return to the principles when they can.

The purpose of the leader self-assessment is not to track compliance. It is to support the kind of honest reflection that helps leaders grow — to name what went well, what fell short, and what they want to do differently. It is a tool for self-awareness, not a performance metric.

Leaders who hold themselves to these principles imperfectly but honestly are practicing something more durable than leaders who perform perfect adherence while privately abandoning the values underneath. The staff will notice the difference. Over time, honest imperfection with genuine effort builds more trust than polished surface compliance.

The Self-Assessment — A Tool for Honest Reflection

The leader self-assessment is organized into two sections:

  • Section A asks about what you actually did — your leadership actions in communication, workload protection, staff support, consistency, and recognition
  • Section B asks about what staff are likely experiencing — whether the culture feels safe, whether voice has effect, whether commitments are being kept

The most important signal is the gap between A and B. High Section A scores alongside low Section B scores mean you are trying — but the impact is not reaching staff. That gap is the most useful thing to reflect on, because closing it requires understanding what is getting in the way.

Questions Worth Sitting With

  • What did I do this week — or this month — that most reflected the values in this document?
  • Where did I fall short, and was that primarily about capacity, about a system failure, or about a choice I made?
  • Do the staff I work with feel seen, supported, and trusted? How do I know — not assume — that?
  • What would I want to do differently if I had more capacity? What small version of that is possible right now?
  • Who on my team may be struggling in a way I haven’t checked on? What’s keeping me from doing that?
An Honest Accounting at Year-End

At the end of each school year, leaders are asked to reflect on the full arc: where they showed up for their staff, where they fell short, what the year taught them, and what they want to carry forward. This reflection is shared within the leadership team — not as a performance evaluation, but as a contribution to organizational learning. The question is not ‘did I follow the plan?’ It is ‘did I hold onto what matters, especially when it was hard?’

These ten principles are not a complete account of everything that matters in school leadership. They are the values this organization is most committed to practicing — imperfectly, honestly, and over time.

Return to them at the start of each year. Return to them in the middle of a hard stretch. Return to them at the end, when you are taking stock of what kind of leader you were this year — and deciding who you want to be next year.
